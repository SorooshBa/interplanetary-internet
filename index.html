01 · THE PROBLEM

Why the internet breaks in space

The internet you use every day was designed for a world where a signal crosses the planet in a fraction of a second. Put a few million kilometers in the way and its basic assumptions fall apart.

Distance creates delay

Signals travel at a finite speed. The farther away the other end is, the longer every message takes — no matter how good your equipment is.

Latency is not bandwidth

Bandwidth is how much data you can send per second. Latency is how long the first bit takes to arrive. Engineers keep increasing bandwidth. They cannot reduce the latency that distance imposes.

Conversations become correspondence

Around the Moon you can still talk, with a pause. At Mars you exchange messages, like email. At another star you write letters.

Six words you'll need

Latency
The time it takes for data to get from one place to another.
One-way delay (OWD)
Latency in one direction. In space, this is the light travel time.
Round-trip time (RTT)
The time for a message to reach the destination and for the reply to come back. About twice the one-way delay.
Bandwidth
How many bits per second a link can carry.
Packet
A small chunk of data sent across a network.
Light-time
The time light needs to travel a given distance. It is the best latency physics will ever allow.

04 · THE SPEED OF LIGHT

299,792 kilometers per second

That number decides everything on this page. It is the fastest speed anything can travel — and the reason a message to Mars can never arrive in under three minutes.

05 · THE COSMIC SPEED LIMIT

Why nothing is faster than light

“Just build a faster connection” solves every other problem in networking. Here is why it can't solve this one.

The energy wall

At everyday speeds, more energy means more speed. Near light speed the rules change: the faster you go, the more energy every extra bit of speed costs. Push anything with mass toward light speed and the energy needed grows without limit.

Light itself has no mass, so it needs no energy to 'get up to speed' — it always travels at exactly c. That is also why you can't make a signal out of massive things (a rocket, a letter, an electron) and beat it.

If messages could outrun light…

Even if you somehow got past the energy wall, there is a deeper problem: a message faster than light would let some observers see the reply arrive before the question was asked. Step through the story.

Ideas that sound like loopholes

Science fiction is full of ways around the light barrier. Here is what physics says about each.

Can't carry messages

Quantum entanglement

Entangled particles share correlated results, but each side's results look completely random on their own. You can't choose what your partner measures, so you can't encode a message. The pattern only shows up when both sides compare notes — over an ordinary, light-speed channel. Physicists call this the no-communication theorem.

Speculative

Wormholes and warp drives

Some solutions of Einstein's equations allow shortcuts through space, but they need exotic matter with negative energy that has never been observed. And a usable shortcut could also bring back the causality paradox.

Never observed

Tachyons

Tachyons are hypothetical particles that always travel faster than light. No experiment has ever found one, and theories that include them run into serious problems.

It was a bug

The 2011 faster-than-light neutrinos

In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos arriving slightly early. In 2012 the team traced the result to a faulty fiber-optic cable connection and a clock problem — a networking bug, of all things. Later measurements agreed with light speed.

Not a loophole

Light is slower in glass

Light slows down inside materials — about 32% slower in optical fiber. Particles can outrun light inside a material (that's the blue glow in reactor pools, called Cherenkov radiation), but nothing outruns light in a vacuum — which is what space is.

Not a loophole

Galaxies receding faster than light

Very distant galaxies recede faster than light because the space between us is expanding, not because they fly through space that fast. Nobody can use this stretching to send a message faster than light.

Questions about c

Why is it called c?

From the Latin celeritas, meaning swiftness.

Is c only about light?

No. c is the speed of everything that has no mass — light, and also gravitational waves — and it is the universal speed limit for cause and effect. Light just happens to be the thing we noticed first.

Is c always the same?

In a vacuum, yes — for every observer, no matter how fast that observer is moving. That strange fact is the starting point of Einstein's special relativity (1905).

06 · HOW IT REALLY WORKS

Building an internet that waits

You can't beat the delay, so real engineers design around it. NASA, ESA and other space agencies are deploying a different kind of network: one that expects delays and disconnections.

Why everyday protocols struggle

Loading a simple web page on Earth takes several back-and-forth exchanges: a DNS lookup, a TCP handshake, a TLS handshake, and finally the HTTP request. Each costs a full round trip. Fine at 20 milliseconds. Not fine at 25 minutes.

What else breaks?

  • TCP's retransmission timers expect round trips measured in milliseconds, so it would keep re-sending data that is merely still on its way.
  • Congestion control learns from acknowledgments that arrive far too late to be useful.
  • Everything assumes a continuous end-to-end path. Planets rotate, orbiters pass overhead and the Sun gets in the way — the path comes and goes.
  • Servers, DNS and databases live on Earth. Every lookup would cross the void.

DTN: the postal service of space

Delay/Disruption-Tolerant Networking (DTN) is a family of protocols built for these conditions. Its core idea is the bundle: a self-contained message that carries everything a node needs to forward it. Instead of demanding a live end-to-end connection, each node keeps the bundle in storage until the next hop becomes available — then sends it on. This is called store-and-forward. Think of the postal system instead of a phone call.

The Bundle Protocol (version 7 is described in RFC 9171) moves bundles between nodes. The Licklider Transmission Protocol (LTP, RFC 5326) handles reliable delivery across a single long-delay link. Both are named for ideas that go back to 1963, when J. C. R. Licklider wrote about an “Intergalactic Computer Network.”

The hardware that makes it work

Deep Space Network

NASA's Deep Space Network uses giant radio antennas at three sites: Goldstone (California), Madrid (Spain) and Canberra (Australia). They are spaced roughly a third of the way around the planet, so as Earth rotates at least one of them can always see a given spacecraft.

Relay orbiters

Mars rovers are small. They upload data by UHF radio to orbiters that pass overhead for around ten minutes at a time. The orbiters have bigger antennas and more power, and relay the data to Earth. It is already a two-hop interplanetary network.

Laser communication

Radio waves spread out; laser beams stay narrow, so far more data fits through. In 2013 a laser link from lunar orbit (LLCD) delivered 622 Mbps. In December 2023 the Deep Space Optical Communications experiment on the Psyche spacecraft sent data at 267 Mbps from about 31 million km — including a short video of a cat named Taters. Lasers raise bandwidth. They do not change the delay.

Autonomy

A 25-minute round trip means nobody can joystick a rover. Controllers upload a plan for the day; the rover carries it out on its own and reports back. Autonomy is a networking feature too.

When the Sun gets in the way

About every 26 months, Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from Earth — a solar conjunction. Radio signals that pass close to the Sun are scrambled by its hot, turbulent gas. For roughly two weeks, missions stop sending commands and rely on plans uploaded in advance. DTN-style thinking turns this from a crisis into a scheduled gap: queue the bundles, and send them when contact returns.

Six rules for life at a delay

  1. 1

    Send complete thoughts

    One message should contain everything the other side needs.

  2. 2

    Push, don't pull

    Send data that will probably be needed before anyone asks for it.

  3. 3

    Cache close to the user

    Keep copies on relay nodes near the destination.

  4. 4

    Expect disconnection

    Store the message, then forward it when the link returns.

  5. 5

    Schedule around contact windows

    Orbits are predictable, so contacts can be planned in advance.

  6. 6

    Be autonomous

    Don't wait for Earth to make every decision.

Milestones

  1. 1963

    J. C. R. Licklider writes about an “Intergalactic Computer Network.”

  2. 1969

    ARPANET carries its first message.

  3. 1977

    Voyager 1 launches. Nearly 50 years later its signal needs about 23½ hours to reach Earth, and it still sends data at about 160 bits per second with a transmitter of roughly 23 watts.

  4. 1998

    Vint Cerf and engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory begin designing an “Interplanetary Internet.”

  5. 2008

    Delay-tolerant networking is tested in deep space for the first time, aboard the EPOXI spacecraft.

  6. 2012

    Curiosity lands on Mars. The signal that it had begun its descent arrives after 13 min 48 s — the landing is already over.

  7. 2013

    A laser link from lunar orbit delivers 622 Mbps to Earth (LLCD).

  8. 2010s

    DTN begins carrying real data on the International Space Station.

  9. 2023

    The laser experiment on the Psyche spacecraft sends data at 267 Mbps from about 31 million km (DSOC).

  10. Planned

    LunaNet: NASA and partners define an interoperable communication and navigation network for the Moon, built on DTN.
